Greetings! This is the first post on my personal site, so I hope everything is working properly for my post in the Hugo static site.

Social media has long been our default window to the world—a quick way to stay informed and feel connected. Over time, however, as these platforms turned into ad-driven algorithms designed to amplify highlight reels, I found myself doubting my own life. Seeing these curated moments made my own life feel plain, taking a real toll on my emotional and mental well-being—especially while I was studying for my bachelor's degree.

I grew up in a family that wasn't as wealthy as some of my Gen Z peers. In high school, many of my classmates seemed quite well-off—taking trips, going to concerts, buying trendy things whenever they wanted, and always looking stylish and popular. Although my family was self-sufficient, I had little to no disposable income for the things I truly wanted. I missed out on social outings and study tours, and it often felt embarrassing navigating that phase simply because I hadn't yet developed my own sense of style.

_"Things would be better in university, right?"_

At first, things actually did start picking up. I began exploring my own style, buying things I genuinely wanted, and making great friends through course group projects and campus activities. But navigating university life and building a social circle takes money. Where did that money come from? It came from my own hustle—working summer jobs, part-time gigs, and internships. Balancing work alongside a full-time Computer Science workload meant my income was modest, and by the end of each academic year, my savings would run thin.

The pressure only grew over time. University life offers a new level of freedom, giving people time for spontaneous getaways and frequent trips. Soon, my Instagram feed was bombarded with endless stories of friends' vacations, group gatherings, high-end hobbies, and posts that felt like subtle displays of wealth. Even though I used the app for practical things—like discovering outfits, looking up hairstyles, or saving posts of things I knew I might never own—I ended up spending most of my daily energy on it.

I became increasingly emotional. When you constantly compare your daily reality to everyone else's highlight reels, you simply can't win. I felt like a loser—carrying all the pressure of daily life and working hard just to stay afloat, while watching others enjoy life effortlessly and doubting myself in the process. My friends were talking about their upcoming trips, concerts, and credit card perks, while I was simply fighting to manage my daily expenses and keep up with my studies. I was losing my happiness to endless comparison.

The breaking point came after graduation. After months of putting in relentless effort, I was still struggling to land a full-time software developer role. Facing five straight months of ghosting and job rejections was mentally exhausting on its own, but seeing social media algorithmically feed me everyone else's career highlights and lifestyle flexes made it unbearable. That was when the toxicity of social media showed its true colors: it wasn't keeping me connected; it was fueling self-doubt when I was at my most vulnerable. Social media was tricking me to scroll indefinitely and forcing me into relentless comparisons. Instagram had betrayed my trust.

At that point, I was trapped in a complete doubt spiral:

> _I feel down..._
>
> _I scroll through my Instagram feed..._
>
> _I see someone's highlight post or story..._
> 
> _It makes me even more emotional..._
>
> _I can't stop scrolling..._
>
> _And the cycle continues._


I was fed up and ultimately decided to deactivate my Instagram account, stopping social media from consuming my time,focus, and mental peace.

At first, I thought about simply muting my friends' posts and stories, but that proved insufficient—Instagram would still serve me recommended content designed to trigger constant comparison. However, I kept Threads active: its text-focused nature means people spend more time discussing ideas and sharing thoughts rather than showing off visual highlights.

I am so glad I deactivated my Instagram account. I feel lighter, calmer, and in control of my time and focus again. With no more endless comparisons or visual noise, I got my energy back to invest in things I can actually control right now:beating games I never had time to finish, building side projects to strengthen my programming skills and portfolio, and exploring new places I've never been. I enjoy living in the moment now, and when I want to catch up with friends, I message them directly on WhatsApp or just simply call them instead.

There is nothing left to worry about, and no algorithmic feed telling me what I should care about. While I might come back to Instagram sometime in the future, this break has made me permanently more wary and intentional about how I use social media.
